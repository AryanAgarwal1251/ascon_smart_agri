\begin{abstract}
A smart farm is a cyber-physical system: a soil-moisture reading decides whether a pump starts, so
a corrupted value wastes water rather than merely misleading a chart. Two requirements follow
together --- the gateway must detect attacks locally, since rural backhaul cannot carry every
packet to the cloud, and must protect the readings that survive inspection. Federated learning
answers the first, but introduces an exposure rarely examined: the parameters now cross the same
untrusted network the detector protects, both ways, every round. Nor does prior work say what the
verdict should do to the data path.

We present and measure a system treating these as one problem. A gated recurrent detector runs on
each gateway; every parameter vector crossing to or from the aggregator is sealed with
Ascon-AEAD128 (NIST SP~800-232) under per-client, per-direction keys whose associated data binds
client, round, direction and schema, so a frame cannot be replayed, reflected or reattributed. The
verdict then selects between paths disjoint in the object graph rather than by convention: benign
readings leave over TLS, malicious readings have no network endpoint at all.

On CICIoT2023 the centralised detector reaches macro-F1 $0.8297 \pm 0.0013$, recurrence
contributing $+0.2322$ over an identical-budget perceptron. Federating three farms recovers 82.4\%
of the local-to-pooled gap, and every farm scores better federated than alone: one whose partition
lacked attack classes rises from $0.6145$ to $0.8145$, detecting what it never saw because another
farm did. Across $K \in \{3,\dots,50\}$ aggregate macro-F1 falls only $0.079$ while the weakest
class collapses $0.642 \rightarrow 0.386$ --- a fall the aggregate conceals. Validated
software-in-the-loop: 120 sealed frames, zero rejections, and 4{,}239 malicious readings with none
reaching the cloud.

Two negative results are contributions. Zero-shot cross-corpus transfer collapses, replicating a
field-wide failure that schema standardisation does not fix; and a safety property can pass
\emph{vacuously}, as one of our own runs did on a single malicious reading. The detector consumes
held-out flow records, so these results establish architectural correctness, not detection on live
farm traffic.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
A smart farm runs on readings that trigger physical actions: a moisture value decides
whether a pump starts. Two security requirements follow, and they arrive together rather than in
sequence. The farm's edge gateway has to recognise attack traffic from compromised devices
locally, because rural backhaul cannot carry every packet to a cloud inspection service. It also
has to protect the readings that survive inspection, since a modified value arriving silently does
more damage than one merely observed.

Each gateway, however, sees only its own traffic. A single farm encounters too few attack
instances to train a detector that generalises, and the rarer families may never appear on its
network at all. Pooling records centrally fixes the statistics and breaks both the bandwidth
constraint and the data-ownership one. Federated learning is the standard answer: exchange
parameters instead of records.

That answer introduces a channel the literature rarely examines. The parameter vectors now cross
the same untrusted field network the detector exists to protect, in both directions, every round.
A curious aggregator or a network observer sees them. Securing that exchange is the problem this
paper takes up, and it is distinct from securing the telemetry: one cipher per hop, each with its
own stated threat.

A second seam runs alongside it. Detection work in this domain terminates at a label, and
cryptographic work benchmarks ciphers on channels that carry no verdict. Neither asks what the
detector's output should \emph{do}. We make the verdict a routing decision over two paths that are
disjoint in the object graph rather than by convention: benign readings leave over TLS, malicious
readings never leave at all.

This paper reports a built and measured system rather than a design. Everything below rests on
committed run manifests, and every figure is generated from them.

\subsection{Contributions}
This paper is an empirical study of a built and measured system. Its contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{An Ascon-sealed bidirectional weight channel.} Every client update and every
  global-state download is an AEAD frame under a per-client, per-direction key, with associated
  data binding the client identity, round index, direction and schema version
  (Eq.~\eqref{eq:weightad}). The aggregator never applies a frame that opens to $\bot$. We
  report 120 sealed frames across 20 rounds with zero rejections and a measured
  271{,}883 bytes per client per round.
  \item \textbf{A per-client measurement of what federating is worth}, rather than a global
  average: every farm improves, and a farm whose partition lacked attack classes still gains
  $0.2000$ macro-F1 from the others' parameters.
  \item \textbf{A client-count study} over 21 configurations that separates aggregate
  degradation from rare-class collapse, and locates the onset of the second descent at the
  pigeonhole bound implied by the rarest class's block count (Eq.~\eqref{eq:pigeonhole}).
  \item \textbf{Dataset selection decided by measurement.} Three corpora were characterised and
  trained on; two were rejected, and the measurements that rejected them are reported rather
  than the choice merely asserted.
  \item \textbf{Software-in-the-loop validation of the full topology} before hardware exists,
  including the configuration errors that made two earlier runs invalid.
  \item \textbf{Two negative results}, one replicating a field-wide generalisation failure and
  one methodological: a safety property reported as satisfied carries no information unless the
  number of opportunities to violate it is reported with it.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background and Related Work}

\subsection{Ascon and Ascon-AEAD128}\label{sec:ascon}
Ascon is a family of lightweight authenticated-encryption and hashing algorithms designed by
Dobraunig, Eichlseder, Mendel and Schl\"affer~\cite{dobraunig2021ascon}. It is built from a
single 320-bit permutation, applied as five 64-bit words through a substitution--permutation
round of constant addition, a 5-bit S-box and a linear diffusion layer, in a sponge-based duplex
mode: the state is initialised from the key, nonce and an algorithm-specific initial value,
associated data is absorbed, plaintext is absorbed and ciphertext squeezed block by block, and a
tag is extracted at finalisation. Ascon-AEAD128 runs that permutation for 12 rounds at
initialisation and finalisation and 8 rounds while absorbing, at a rate of 128 bits against a
192-bit capacity. The same permutation serves every stage, and it is used only in the forward
direction, so no inverse need be implemented --- which is what makes the primitive small enough
for a constrained device and simple enough to mask against side channels. In 2019 Ascon-128 and
Ascon-128a were selected as the first choice for the lightweight authenticated-encryption use
case in the final CAESAR portfolio.

In August 2025 NIST published SP~800-232~\cite{nist2025sp800232}, standardising
\textbf{Ascon-AEAD128} together with Ascon-Hash256, Ascon-XOF128 and Ascon-CXOF128. This system
uses Ascon-AEAD128 exclusively: a 128-bit key, a 128-bit nonce and an untruncated 128-bit tag.

One implementation detail is worth stating because it is a trap. \emph{The standardised
Ascon-AEAD128 is not the pre-standard Ascon-128 or Ascon-128a.} It is based on Ascon-128a, but
the standard changes seven things, and two of them break interoperability silently: the initial
values were reformatted, and \textbf{the byte order was switched from big-endian to
little-endian} to suit little-endian microcontrollers. A widely installed Python package named
\texttt{ascon} implements only the pre-standard variants, and a system built on it would
interoperate with nothing that follows the standard while passing its own round-trip tests ---
because a round-trip test encrypts and decrypts with the same wrong constants. We therefore bind
the designers' own C implementations~\cite{asconc} and gate all cryptographic integration behind
conformance to the standard's known-answer vectors, whose provenance is the Cryptographic
Algorithm Validation Program that SP~800-232 itself names~\cite{nistcavp}. That gate is asserted
by a test which must pass before any other cryptographic code is exercised. No primitive is
implemented from scratch in this work.

\subsection{What the standard requires of a design that uses many keys}\label{sec:multikey}
Two conformance requirements bear directly on an architecture that, like this one, issues a
separate key per client and per direction rather than sharing one.

\textbf{Multi-key security has a price, and it is small.} SP~800-232 states that with $u$
independently selected keys and no tag truncation, Ascon-AEAD128 provides
\begin{equation}
s(u) = 128 - \log_2 u \quad \text{bits}
\label{eq:multikey}
\end{equation}
of security for both confidentiality and integrity in the nonce-respecting setting. Our design
holds one key per client per direction, so $u = 2K$ and the cost of federating is
$\log_2 2K$ bits: $125.4$ bits at $K=3$, and still $121.4$ bits at the $K=50$ of
Section~\ref{sec:ksweep}. \emph{Scaling the federation by a factor of sixteen costs four bits of
security.} The standard also offers a nonce-masking option that restores the full 128 bits in
the multi-key setting; we do not use it, because it requires a second 128-bit key per channel and
forfeits context-commitment security, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:multikey} shows there is nothing here
worth buying back.

\textbf{The key-lifetime limit is not reachable by this system.} The standard caps the data
processed under one key at $2^{54}$ bytes and requires a key update at that point. At the
$135{,}942$ bytes per client per direction per round measured in Section~\ref{sec:commcost}, a
key would reach the limit after $1.3\times10^{11}$ rounds --- some $6.6\times10^{9}$ runs of the
twenty-round schedule used throughout this paper. Rekeying is therefore a requirement the design
satisfies by never approaching it, which is worth stating precisely rather than leaving a
reviewer to wonder. The companion limit on decryption failures, $2^{96}$ for an untruncated tag,
is likewise far above the zero rejections we observe; the aggregator counts them because a
nonzero count is a signal, not because the bound is in play.

\subsection{What Ascon protects here, and what it does not}
Ascon protects \emph{the model parameters}, in both directions, between each local detector and
the aggregator. It does not protect the telemetry on its way to the cloud; that hop uses TLS.
The reasoning is one cipher per hop with a stated threat for each: the parameter exchange
traverses the untrusted field network and is the channel this work introduces, whereas the
gateway-to-cloud hop is an ordinary authenticated transport problem already solved by TLS.
The three channels, and which adversary each faces, are set out in
Section~\ref{sec:threat}.

\subsection{Thread A: benchmark data and sequence-based detection}
CICIoT2023~\cite{neto2023ciciot} is the corpus this work trains on. It was built on a testbed of
105 real IoT devices and contains roughly 46.7~million labelled records with 46--47 flow-derived
features, spanning one benign class and 33 attack classes grouped into families. Two of its
properties drive the design.

Each record summarises a fixed-size \emph{packet window} rather than a single packet. The data is
therefore already aggregated once over time, and a recurrent model reading a sequence of records
performs a second-order temporal analysis: it observes how flow statistics evolve. That is the
justification for recurrence here, and it rests on the record semantics rather than on the
general observation that network traffic is sequential.

The class distribution is severely imbalanced, with a reported imbalance ratio near $5751$,
so a classifier predicting the majority class alone attains high accuracy while detecting nothing
of interest. Any evaluation on this corpus reporting accuracy alone is uninformative, which is
why macro-F1 and false-positive rate are treated as primary throughout.

Recurrent and convolutional-recurrent architectures dominate the applied
literature~\cite{okey2024cnngru,bai2025ietinfsec}. S12~\cite{bai2025ietinfsec} is the useful
dissent: benchmarking nine methods, it found a random forest strongest on tabular flow features,
then compared simple, weighted and difference-based aggregation rules federated over that model.

\textbf{Limitation across Thread A.} Papers building sequences from flow records frequently
shuffle rows before windowing, or do not state the ordering procedure at all. Where the source
data carries no timestamp or flow identifier, a sliding window is a \emph{positional} construct
and not a temporal one. We found this distinction rarely acknowledged; it is the
sequence-construction gap of Section~\ref{sec:gaps}.

\subsection{Thread B: federated learning for IoT intrusion detection}
Federated averaging~\cite{mcmahan2017fedavg} is the reference algorithm: clients perform local
stochastic gradient descent and the server averages parameters weighted by local sample count.
FedProx~\cite{li2020fedprox} adds a proximal term constraining client drift under statistical
heterogeneity, and is the standard citation for why non-identically distributed client data
degrades plain FedAvg. Broader context comes from a survey of federated learning for
IoT~\cite{nguyen2021flsurvey} and a systematic review of federated intrusion
detection~\cite{hernandezramos2025fedidsreview}.

Applied results are consistent in direction. Federated deep detectors reach accuracy close to
their centralised counterparts on IoT
benchmarks~\cite{belarbi2023federated,karunamurthy2025optimal}, including on CICIoT2023 itself.
Work on the industrial IoT quantifies the privacy--utility trade-off when differential privacy is
added~\cite{ruzafa2021ppfl}. Alternative
architectures~\cite{abdelaziz2025tabtransformer} and privacy-preserving
strategies~\cite{khraisat2025discoveriot} fill out the space.

S10~\cite{okey2024cnngru} is the closest prior work to our detection layer. It builds a federated
ensemble over CNN, GRU and LSTM with two aggregation functions, weighted FedAvg and weighted
FedProx, evaluated on CICIoT2023 and FLNET2023; CNN-GRU reaches 98.25\% accuracy on CICIoT2023 and
LSTM-GRU 93.36\% and 95.66\% on the two corpora respectively. It establishes that GRU plus
weighted federated averaging on CICIoT2023 is workable, which de-risks our detection design. It
does not consider what happens to traffic after classification, does not model a runtime IoT
stream, and is not set in an agricultural context.

\textbf{Limitations across Thread B.} Four recur, and they become four of the gaps in
Section~\ref{sec:gaps}. Privacy is asserted rather than bounded, many papers treating ``raw data
does not move'' as equivalent to privacy, which parameter-inversion attacks show it is not.
Client partitioning is under-specified, and a uniform random split is often described as
heterogeneous when it is identically distributed by construction. The local-only baseline is
usually absent, although it is the comparison that matters operationally, since local-only is what
an operator gets by declining to federate. And the pipeline terminates at a label.

\subsection{Thread C: lightweight authenticated encryption in deployment}
Performance evidence for Ascon on constrained platforms is substantial. An ASIC
realisation~\cite{khan2024asconasic} and benchmarking on AI-enabled IoT
devices~\cite{khan2022asconperf} establish suitability for field-node cost budgets. There are
also direct evaluations over IoT application protocols. LWE-IoT~\cite{suleiman2026lweiot} compares
Ascon, PRESENT, SIMON, SPECK and ChaCha20 over both MQTT and CoAP on ESP32 nodes across payload
sizes, which is the protocol-level measurement methodology closest to our own.

El-Hajj and Gebremariam~\cite{elhajj2024asconindustry40} validate Ascon against AES-GCM over MQTT
in an Industry~4.0 digital-twin setting, reporting lower execution time for Ascon and a memory
footprint roughly 1~KB larger in RAM and Flash, against approximately 8~KB additional RAM and
5.6~KB additional Flash for AES-GCM at equivalent security. Those figures bear directly on the
constrained-device argument of Section~\ref{sec:threat}.

A note on scope, because it is easy to over-read this literature. Öztürk et
al.~\cite{ozturk2025chaosascon} integrate keys and nonces from a Zaslavsky chaotic map to build a
\emph{chaos-based Ascon variant}, claiming higher security than standard Ascon, and test it on
Raspberry~Pi~3B hardware with ROS~2 robots. That is useful evidence that Ascon-class
cryptography runs on gateway hardware of the kind this work targets. It is \emph{not} evidence
about standardised Ascon-AEAD128 over MQTT, and we do not cite it as such.

\textbf{Limitation across Thread C.} These evaluations benchmark the cipher on a channel that
carries no intelligence about whether the traffic should be forwarded at all. Encryption and
detection are treated as independent layers --- the verdict-routing gap.

\subsection{Thread D: agricultural and application-layer security}
Work specific to agriculture confirms both the relevance of lightweight cryptography and the
shape of the deployment. Fathy and Ali~\cite{fathy2023irrigation} secure an IoT irrigation system
for precision agriculture, protecting published sensor data and the irrigation decisions returned
to actuators, and reporting improvements in power consumption, execution time and memory over
AES. It uses a non-standardised cipher and contains no detection layer, but it establishes the
threat scenario this work adopts for the gateway-to-cloud channel: an adversary who alters sensor
readings in transit changes what the farm does.

Two agricultural corpora deserve mention even though neither is used here.
Edge-IIoTset~\cite{ferrag2022edgeiiotset} is the reference IIoT benchmark and includes
agricultural sensing over MQTT; Section~\ref{sec:semantics} reports what we measured on it and why
it was excluded. Farm-Flow~\cite{ferreira2025farmflow} is a real agricultural IoT testbed of over a
million network flows whose attack set includes MQTT Flood, an attack on the protocol our sensor
nodes actually speak and one that neither CIC corpus contains.

\subsection{Thread E: cross-corpus generalisation}\label{sec:thread_e}
A fifth thread, absent from the original review and added because our own results demanded it,
concerns whether a detector trained on one corpus works on another.

The finding is consistent and negative. Layeghy and
Portmann~\cite{layeghy2022generalisability} convert four NIDS corpora to a \emph{single}
standardised 43-feature NetFlow schema~\cite{sarhan2021standardfeature} --- removing feature-schema
mismatch as an explanation --- and still measure an average 56.28\% performance decay when a
supervised model trained on one is evaluated on another, with scores sometimes falling near zero.
Their conclusion is that ``none of the considered models is able to generalise over all studied
datasets.'' The failure is also strongly asymmetric: one model scores 94.83\% in one direction and
4.90\% reversed. A SHAP analysis attributes high within-corpus performance to shortcut features
that do not transfer. The same failure is documented for IIoT~\cite{hakim2026crossdomain}, and with a
concrete mechanism: training four lightweight models on one industrial corpus and testing on two
structurally different ones, the strongest models are found to lean on port-category features
whose rate in source-domain attack traffic exceeds the target domain's by factors of
\textbf{96 to 435}. That is the same shortcut the SHAP analysis above identifies, measured
directly. A survey of 89 NIDS corpora~\cite{goldschmidt2025datasetsurvey} lists weak
cross-dataset generalisation among the field's standing failure modes.

It is worth noting that the diagnosis and the leading proposed remedy come from the same group:
the authors of~\cite{layeghy2022generalisability} also propose domain-invariant feature
extraction~\cite{layeghy2023dinids}. The problem is recognised by the people best placed to
measure it, and remains open.

What does work is combining corpora during training rather than hoping for zero-shot transfer. The
closest published work to this paper's setting~\cite{dataset_centric_fl_ids} evaluates a federated
IDS across three IoT corpora and reports up to 30 percentage points of macro-F1 lost cross-corpus,
but approximately 90\% recovered under combined multi-corpus federated training --- which is the
design this work arrived at independently. Two of their secondary findings bear noting: FedNova
reduces communication by 15--25\% against FedAvg, and a Transformer backbone gains 1--2 points of
macro-F1 over an LSTM. This work nonetheless uses weighted FedAvg and a GRU: FedAvg is the
baseline Eq.~\eqref{eq:fedavg} defines and the one an operator can reason about, and a GRU is the
cheapest recurrent option for a gateway-class device, which Section~\ref{sec:sil} shows matters.

Domain-adversarial training is the principled alternative. DI-NIDS~\cite{layeghy2023dinids} extracts
domain-invariant features adversarially and detects on them with a one-class SVM, reporting
superior cross-domain performance on standardised NetFlow corpora. It requires several source
domains, which this work does not have.

Finally, Galal~\cite{galal2026provenance} is directly relevant twice over. It shows that four of
the seven columns Edge-IIoTset's official preprocessing recipe one-hot encodes separate attack
from normal traffic with accuracy $1.0000$ \emph{on their own}, through the spelling of a
placeholder for an absent protocol field --- a serialisation artefact encoding file provenance
rather than behaviour --- and that five of six standard classifiers consequently reach exactly
$1.0000 \pm 0.0000$. Under a corrected protocol the strongest model settles at $0.9503 \pm
0.0011$. It then rebuilds the benchmark from raw captures with full per-device attribution and
runs a \emph{leave-one-device-out} sweep, locating the generalisation boundary at the
perception/actuation layer where a random forest falls from $0.9988$ to $0.5083$ balanced
accuracy. Leave-one-device-out is the generalisation question a federated farm deployment actually
faces --- a new site is unseen devices, not an unseen testbed --- and
Section~\ref{sec:limitations} states why this work could not run it.

\subsection{Patent landscape}
Three filings bound the space. A granted US patent~\cite{patent_us12301597} trains a random-forest
classifier on CICIoT2023 features at the network edge using a digital-twin representation; it is
the closest granted patent by corpus and placement, but training is centralised and there is no
payload cryptography. An Indian filing~\cite{patent_vit_fl} claims collaborative training of
intrusion-detection models without sharing traffic, applying differential-privacy noise to
parameters before transmission and aggregating with FedAvg --- adjacent to our federation layer,
and adding the differential privacy we place out of scope. A further filing claims
privacy-preserving global threat detection with standardised threat-intelligence
dissemination~\cite{patent_mallareddy}, establishing prior art for federation across heterogeneous
device tiers.

The conclusion is narrow. Federated intrusion detection is actively claimed, and edge detection on
CICIoT2023 is actively claimed. What the surveyed landscape does not contain is an architecture in
which a standardised lightweight-AEAD channel is conditioned on the output of a federated
detector. The components are well covered; the composition appears unclaimed.

\subsection{Research gaps}\label{sec:gaps}
The review exposes six gaps. We name them rather than number them, because each recurs
throughout the paper.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The verdict-routing gap.} Intrusion detection and payload protection are studied
  as separate problems, so no prior work specifies what the detector's verdict should do to the
  data path. Our response is Eq.~\eqref{eq:route}, and paths that are disjoint in the object
  graph rather than by convention (Section~\ref{sec:routing}).
  \item \textbf{The unbounded-privacy-claim gap.} Federated IDS work asserts privacy because raw
  records are not transmitted, without bounding what the transmitted parameters leak. We restrict
  our claim to exactly that---raw records are not transmitted---and name gradient inversion and
  poisoning as unaddressed.
  \item \textbf{The partition-specification gap.} Client partitions are under-specified and
  usually IID, so federated results are neither reproducible nor representative of deployment.
  We partition by a Dirichlet draw with $\alpha$ as an experimental variable
  (Eq.~\eqref{eq:dirichlet}) and publish the realised per-client histograms.
  \item \textbf{The missing-lower-bound gap.} Without a local-only baseline a federated result
  has no lower bound, and the benefit of federating is unquantified. The three-way comparison is
  a primary result (Section~\ref{sec:fedresults}).
  \item \textbf{The sequence-construction gap.} Window ordering, label homogeneity and labelling
  rules are left unstated, so sequence models are not comparable across papers. We state ours as
  assumptions and defend them (Section~\ref{sec:sequences}).
  \item \textbf{The feature-provenance gap.} A detector trained on flow-derived features cannot
  consume an application-layer payload, and runtime demonstrations do not say which features the
  model actually consumed. We define the boundary explicitly and state what the demonstration
  does and does not show (Section~\ref{sec:provenance}).
\end{enumerate}

Two further gaps are ours, both found by measurement rather than by reading:

\begin{enumerate}\setcounter{enumi}{6}
  \item \textbf{The vacuous-verification gap.} Safety properties are reported as satisfied
  without reporting how many opportunities to violate them arose, so a passing assertion can
  carry no information (Section~\ref{sec:vacuous}).
  \item \textbf{The cross-corpus semantics gap.} Corpora are combined on column \emph{names}, so
  a shared schema can conceal incompatible quantities (Section~\ref{sec:semantics}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Threat Model and System Architecture}\label{sec:threat}

\subsection{The deployment environment}
Smart agriculture places networked sensing and actuation across farmland: soil-moisture probes,
temperature and humidity sensors, water-level and flow meters, and programmable irrigation
controllers, publishing over MQTT to a farm-side gateway. This is a cyber-physical system, not a
data-collection network, and the distinction drives everything that follows. A corrupted
soil-moisture reading does not produce a misleading chart; it causes a pump to run, or to stay off
when it should not. The damage is physical, appears days later, and is expensive. Integrity
therefore matters at least as much as confidentiality.

Four properties of the field environment shape the design.

\textbf{C1 --- Resource constraints.} Field nodes are battery- or solar-powered
microcontrollers whose energy budget for cryptography and inference is measured in microjoules.
This is the device class that motivated the NIST lightweight cryptography programme.

\textbf{C2 --- Constrained backhaul.} Rural connectivity is cellular or long-range radio, metered
and intermittent. Continuously shipping raw captures to a central service for training is not
bandwidth-feasible, a claim quantified in Section~\ref{sec:commcost}.

\textbf{C3 --- Physical exposure.} Nodes sit in open fields where an adversary can approach them
or capture radio traffic with commodity hardware. The assumption of a trusted internal network,
reasonable inside a building, does not hold.

\textbf{C4 --- Data ownership.} Farm operational data is commercially sensitive because it
predicts yield, and cooperatives are increasingly unwilling to hand raw records to a third-party
aggregator.

\subsection{Three channels, three adversaries}
We distinguish three channels, each with a different adversary, because conflating them is a
common source of overstated security claims.

\textbf{Channel 1: device-to-gateway traffic.} The adversary is a compromised IoT device on the
same network --- the scenario the corpus was built to represent. Table~\ref{tab:families} gives
the attack families and what each means for a farm. \emph{The defence is detection.}

\textbf{Channel 2: gateway-to-cloud telemetry.} The adversary is a network observer who can read,
modify, replay or drop messages in transit. Detection does not help, because the traffic has
already been judged benign. \emph{The defence is authenticated encryption}, and confidentiality
alone is insufficient: a scheme without integrity hands the receiver a modified moisture reading
indistinguishable from a genuine one.

\textbf{Channel 3: node-to-aggregator model updates.} The adversary is either a curious
aggregator that would reconstruct client data, or a compromised client submitting a poisoned
update. \emph{This is the channel this work introduces}, and the one Ascon protects here. Federated
learning addresses the first threat only partially and the second not at all; both are named as
unaddressed in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Formal problem statement}\label{sec:formal}
Let $\mathcal{D} = \{(x_t, y_t)\}_{t=1}^{M}$ be a corpus of network flow records, $x_t \in
\mathbb{R}^{F_0}$ a vector of flow-derived features and $y_t \in \mathcal{C}$ a traffic-class
label with $|\mathcal{C}| = C$. After selection to $F \le F_0$ dimensions and windowing with
length $W$, a training example is
\begin{equation}
X_i = \big[\, x_i,\; x_{i+1},\; \dots,\; x_{i+W-1} \,\big]^{\top} \in \mathbb{R}^{W \times F}.
\label{eq:window}
\end{equation}
We seek $f_\theta : \mathbb{R}^{W \times F} \to \Delta^{C-1}$ together with the deterministic
binary projection
\begin{equation}
b(\hat{y}) =
\begin{cases}
\text{benign}, & \hat{y} = c_{\text{benign}},\\
\text{malicious}, & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\qquad \hat{y} = \arg\max_c f_\theta(X)_c .
\label{eq:binproj}
\end{equation}
The parameters are to be obtained without centralising $\mathcal{D}$. With $K$ clients holding
disjoint partitions $\mathcal{D}_1,\dots,\mathcal{D}_K$ of sizes $n_1,\dots,n_K$ and
$n = \sum_k n_k$, the objective is
\begin{equation}
\min_{\theta} F(\theta) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \frac{n_k}{n} F_k(\theta),
\label{eq:fedobj}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
F_k(\theta) = \frac{1}{n_k} \sum_{(X,y) \in \mathcal{D}_k} \ell\big(f_\theta(X),\, y\big),
\label{eq:localobj}
\end{equation}
subject to no element of $\mathcal{D}_k$ being transmitted beyond client $k$. Equation~\eqref{eq:route}
then states the coupling that the verdict-routing gap identifies: the classifier output is consumed
as a control decision over the data path rather than written to a log.

\subsection{Corpus, leakage control, and why one corpus}\label{sec:data}
Before any modelling, a characterisation script reports the real column names and types,
per-column null and zero-variance checks, the exact duplicate count, the label vocabulary with
counts, and the numeric correlation matrix. No column or class name enters the paper unless that
script produced it; figures quoted from the literature are treated as hypotheses to confirm.

\textbf{Subsampling.} The full corpus does not fit in workstation memory, so we draw a stratified
subsample by reading whole capture parts in a fixed order and applying a per-class cap $\kappa_c$,
\begin{equation}
n^{\text{sub}}_c = \min(n_c,\, \kappa_c),
\label{eq:cap}
\end{equation}
chosen to compress the dominant flooding classes while retaining every available instance of the
rare families. The seed and the realised counts go into the run manifest.

\textbf{Deduplication ordering, and why it is a control rather than hygiene.} The corpus contains
exact duplicate rows. If deduplication runs \emph{after} the split, identical records appear on
both sides and the test set measures memorisation. Writing $\delta$ for the duplicate fraction and
$\rho$ for the probability that a duplicate's twin lands in training, the fraction of test items
effectively seen during training is approximately $\delta\rho$, and accuracy is inflated by about
\begin{equation}
\Delta \approx \delta\,\rho\,\big(1 - \text{Acc}_{\text{true}}\big).
\label{eq:leak}
\end{equation}
The inflation scales with $1 - \text{Acc}_{\text{true}}$, so it is \emph{largest for the hardest
classes} --- a detector is flattered most precisely where its performance matters. We therefore fix
the order
\begin{equation}
\text{deduplicate} \;\rightarrow\; \text{split} \;\rightarrow\; \text{fit} \;\rightarrow\;
\text{transform},
\label{eq:order}
\end{equation}
and assert it with a test that fails if any record hash appears in both partitions. Every run
manifest records the realised overlap, which is zero throughout this paper.

\textbf{Taxonomy and splitting.} The task is $C = 8$ classes: benign plus seven attack families.
At the corpus's imbalance ratio
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{IR} = \frac{\max_c n_c}{\min_c n_c} \approx 5751,
\label{eq:ir}
\end{equation}
the rarest leaf classes cannot support a defensible macro-F1 estimate, and the family label is in
any case the actionable granularity for an operator. The binary decision of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:binproj} is a projection of the eight-class output; no second model is trained for
it. Splitting operates on contiguous \emph{blocks} rather than rows, so local ordering survives
into sequence construction, and a stratified subset of blocks forms a global test set shared by
every client and every baseline.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Leakage-controlled preparation}\label{alg:prep}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require raw capture parts $\mathcal{P}$, per-class cap $\kappa$, block size $b$, test fraction $\tau$
\State $D \gets \textsc{StratifiedCappedSubsample}(\mathcal{P}, \kappa)$
\State $D \gets \textsc{Deduplicate}(D)$ \Comment{before splitting, not after}
\State $\beta \gets \textsc{AssignBlocks}(D, b)$ \Comment{blocks never span a label}
\State $(\beta_{\text{tr}}, \beta_{\text{te}}) \gets \textsc{StratifiedBlockSplit}(\beta, \tau)$
\State \textbf{assert} $\textsc{Hashes}(D_{\text{tr}}) \cap \textsc{Hashes}(D_{\text{te}}) = \emptyset$
\State $S \gets \textsc{BuildWindows}(D_{\text{tr}})$ \Comment{within contiguous same-label runs}
\State \Return $(S, D_{\text{te}})$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Feature selection}\label{sec:features}
Subsampling reduces rows; feature selection reduces columns. The selector runs in four
stages, all fitted on training blocks only, and is persisted so that inference uses exactly the
same columns.

\emph{Stage 1, rule-based elimination.} Drop labels, identifiers, zero-variance columns, and any
column that could not exist at inference time.

\emph{Stage 2, correlation pruning.} Compute the Spearman rank correlation $\rho_{jk}$ for every
numeric pair on training data and remove one member of any pair with $|\rho_{jk}| \ge \tau$,
default $\tau = 0.95$, keeping the member with higher univariate relevance.

\emph{Stage 3, relevance ranking.} Score each surviving feature by mutual information with the
label,
\begin{equation}
I(X_j; Y) = \sum_{x \in X_j} \sum_{y \in \mathcal{C}} p(x,y)
\log \frac{p(x,y)}{p(x)\,p(y)},
\label{eq:mi}
\end{equation}
and independently by random-forest impurity importance, then combine the two rankings by
reciprocal rank fusion,
\begin{equation}
s(j) = \sum_{m \in \{\text{MI},\,\text{RF}\}} \frac{1}{\kappa + r_m(j)}, \qquad \kappa = 60,
\label{eq:rrf}
\end{equation}
where $r_m(j)$ is the rank of feature $j$ under method $m$. Two methods are used because they
disagree informatively: mutual information captures non-monotonic univariate dependence, while
impurity importance is biased toward high-cardinality features. Where they disagree we report the
disagreement rather than hide it in an average.

\emph{Stage 4, cardinality selection.} Sweep $F \in \{8, 12, 16, 24, F_0\}$ and take the knee of
the validation macro-F1 curve, which selects $F = 16$ from $F_0 = 39$ canonical columns.

We deliberately do not apply principal component analysis. Components are linear mixtures, which
destroys the column-level interpretability an operator needs in order to act on an alert and would
make the provenance boundary of Section~\ref{sec:provenance} impossible to specify honestly.

\subsection{Temporal sequence construction}\label{sec:sequences}
Windows are built only over maximal runs of adjacent records sharing both label and source
capture. Rows are never shuffled before windowing; only complete sequences are shuffled at batch
formation. A window's label is that of its final record, $y_i = y_{i+W-1}$. The number of
sequences obtainable from runs of lengths $\{L_r\}$ at window $W$ is
\begin{equation}
N_{\text{seq}} = \sum_r \max(0,\; L_r - W + 1).
\label{eq:nseq}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Detection model}
Every client holds an identical architecture, which is a precondition for parameter
averaging. A single gated recurrent layer reads the window and a linear head produces class
logits. For input $x_t \in \mathbb{R}^{F}$ and hidden state $h_{t-1} \in \mathbb{R}^{H}$,
\begin{align}
z_t &= \sigma\big(W_z x_t + U_z h_{t-1} + b_z\big), \label{eq:gru_z}\\
r_t &= \sigma\big(W_r x_t + U_r h_{t-1} + b_r\big), \label{eq:gru_r}\\
\tilde{h}_t &= \tanh\big(W_h x_t + U_h (r_t \odot h_{t-1}) + b_h\big), \label{eq:gru_ht}\\
h_t &= (1 - z_t) \odot h_{t-1} + z_t \odot \tilde{h}_t, \label{eq:gru_h}
\end{align}
with $\sigma$ the logistic function and $\odot$ the elementwise product. The update gate $z_t$ is
what carries evidence across the window: a burst beginning early remains represented in $h_W$.
Class scores follow from the final state,
\begin{equation}
p = \mathrm{softmax}\big(W_o\, \mathrm{LN}(h_W) + b_o\big),
\label{eq:head}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{LN}$ is layer normalisation. That choice over batch normalisation is deliberate and
specific to federation: batch statistics are running estimates of one client's input distribution,
so averaging them across clients with different distributions produces a quantity describing no
client's data. Layer normalisation carries no such cross-client state.

The default configuration has $F=16$ input features, hidden width $H=96$ and $C=8$ classes, giving
\begin{equation}
|\theta| = 3H(F + H + 1) + H(2 + C) + C = 33{,}800,
\label{eq:params}
\end{equation}
a figure asserted by a unit test so that a silent architecture change cannot pass review.

Class imbalance is handled by weighting the cross-entropy from \emph{training} counts only,
\begin{equation}
w_c = \frac{n}{C \, n_c},
\label{eq:classweights}
\end{equation}
with no synthetic oversampling, which would fabricate temporal structure the windowing rules
exist to preserve.

\subsection{Federated training}
Client $k$'s partition is drawn blockwise from a Dirichlet distribution,
\begin{equation}
(p_{1c},\dots,p_{Kc}) \sim \mathrm{Dir}(\alpha \mathbf{1}_K) \quad \text{per class } c,
\label{eq:dirichlet}
\end{equation}
with $\alpha$ an experimental variable rather than a fixed choice. Aggregation is weighted
FedAvg over \emph{sequence} counts, not raw row counts:
\begin{equation}
\theta^{(t+1)} = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \frac{n_k}{\sum_j n_j}\, \theta_k^{(t)},
\qquad n_k = |S_k|.
\label{eq:fedavg}
\end{equation}
Weighting by rows rather than sequences is a common and silent error, because windowing is not
length-preserving; Eq.~\eqref{eq:fedavg} is asserted by a test.

Standardisation is federated: each client contributes only count, mean and second-moment
statistics, combined by Chan's parallel formula,
\begin{align}
n_{AB} &= n_A + n_B, \qquad \delta = \mu_B - \mu_A, \label{eq:chan1}\\
\mu_{AB} &= \mu_A + \delta\,\frac{n_B}{n_{AB}}, \qquad
M_{2,AB} = M_{2,A} + M_{2,B} + \delta^2\frac{n_A n_B}{n_{AB}}. \label{eq:chan2}
\end{align}
No raw record leaves a client, and the federated scaler was measured equal to a pooled scaler to
within $5\times10^{-12}$ at every client count tested.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{One sealed federated round}\label{alg:round}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require global state $\theta^{(t)}$, clients $1..K$, keys $(k^{\uparrow}_i, k^{\downarrow}_i)$
\For{each client $i$ in parallel}
  \State $\theta_i \gets \textsc{LocalTrain}(\theta^{(t)}, S_i, E)$
  \State $a \gets \langle i,\, t,\, \textsf{up},\, v \rangle$;\quad $\nu \gets \textsc{Csprng}(128)$
  \State send $\textsc{Seal}(k^{\uparrow}_i, \nu, a, \textsc{Serialize}(\theta_i, n_i))$
\EndFor
\For{each received frame $f$ from client $i$}
  \State $m \gets \textsc{Open}(k^{\uparrow}_i, f)$
  \State \textbf{if} $m = \bot$ \textbf{then} reject and \textbf{continue} \Comment{never applied}
  \State $(\theta_i, n_i) \gets \textsc{Deserialize}(m)$
\EndFor
\State $\theta^{(t+1)} \gets \sum_i \frac{n_i}{\sum_j n_j}\theta_i$ \Comment{Eq.~\eqref{eq:fedavg}}
\For{each client $i$}
  \State $a' \gets \langle i,\, t,\, \textsf{down},\, v \rangle$;\quad $\nu' \gets \textsc{Csprng}(128)$
  \State send $\textsc{Seal}(k^{\downarrow}_i, \nu', a', \textsc{Serialize}(\theta^{(t+1)}))$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Authenticated encryption construction}\label{sec:aead}
Both protected channels use Ascon-AEAD128 as specified in SP~800-232~\cite{nist2025sp800232},
with a 128-bit key, 128-bit nonce and 128-bit tag. Encryption produces a ciphertext and a tag,
\begin{equation}
(c, \mathrm{t}) = \mathrm{Enc}_{k}(\nu,\, a,\, p),
\label{eq:enc}
\end{equation}
and decryption returns the payload only if the tag verifies:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Dec}_{k}(\nu,\, a,\, c,\, \mathrm{t}) \in \{\, p,\; \bot \,\}.
\label{eq:dec}
\end{equation}
The $\bot$ outcome is the property that matters here. A scheme offering confidentiality alone
would return \emph{some} plaintext for a modified ciphertext, and a farm would act on it.

\textbf{Associated data.} The associated data is authenticated but not encrypted, because a
receiver must read the sender's identity before it can select a key. For telemetry we set
\begin{equation}
a = \langle \texttt{edge\_id},\; \texttt{device\_id},\; \texttt{counter},\;
\texttt{schema\_version} \rangle,
\label{eq:telemetryad}
\end{equation}
and for the weight channel the layout of Eq.~\eqref{eq:weightad}. Authenticating a monotonic
counter also yields replay detection, which encryption alone does not provide.

\textbf{Nonce discipline.} Nonce reuse under a fixed key is catastrophic for AEAD security, so a
fresh 16-byte nonce is drawn per message from a cryptographically secure generator and
transmitted alongside the ciphertext. For $q$ messages under one key the birthday bound gives
\begin{equation}
\Pr[\text{collision}] \le \frac{q(q-1)}{2^{129}},
\label{eq:nonce}
\end{equation}
which for $q = 10^{6}$ is below $1.5 \times 10^{-27}$: random selection suffices at any volume
this system reaches. A test nonetheless accumulates every nonce issued under each key and asserts
zero collisions, because the check costs almost nothing and the failure would be unrecoverable.

\textbf{Overhead.} The wire overhead is a 16-byte tag plus a 16-byte nonce, independent of
payload size,
\begin{equation}
|c|_{\text{wire}} = |p| + 32 \ \text{bytes}.
\label{eq:overhead}
\end{equation}
For a 96-byte sensor reading that is a 33\% expansion, falling to 6.3\% at 512 bytes. The relative
cost is therefore worst exactly where agricultural telemetry sits, which is an argument for
batching several readings per message --- and a measurement we report rather than assume.

\subsection{The sealed weight channel}\label{sec:channel}
Each frame carries associated data
\begin{equation}
a = \langle \texttt{client\_id},\; \texttt{round},\; \texttt{direction},\; \texttt{schema\_version} \rangle,
\label{eq:weightad}
\end{equation}
authenticated but not encrypted, so a frame cannot be replayed into a different round, reflected
into the opposite direction, attributed to a different client, or reinterpreted under a different
parameter layout. Keys are per client and per direction, nonces are drawn fresh from a CSPRNG for
every frame, and parameters are serialised with safetensors rather than pickle so that opening a
frame cannot execute code. Per-round traffic is
\begin{equation}
B = 2K\big(|\theta| s + h\big),
\label{eq:bytes}
\end{equation}
which predicts 811{,}200 bytes per round at $K=3$ against 815{,}136 measured, the
0.5\% excess being safetensors framing.

\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{03_verdict_routing.png}
\caption{The runtime path of a single reading. The payload is never parsed into features: the
model consumes a held-out flow-derived record paired to the message, and nothing is synthesised
(Section~\ref{sec:provenance}). The malicious branch has no network endpoint, because the alert
sink holds no transport that could reach one.}
\label{fig:routing}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Verdict routing with disjoint paths}\label{sec:routing}
The verdict selects the path:
\begin{equation}
\text{path}(x) =
\begin{cases}
\text{TLS to cloud}, & \hat{y}(x) = \text{benign},\\
\text{local alert}, & \hat{y}(x) \neq \text{benign}.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:route}
\end{equation}
Disjointness is enforced in the object graph, not by a coding rule: the handler for malicious
verdicts is constructed with no arguments at all and therefore holds no transport it could reach
the network with. Breaking the property requires changing a constructor signature, which three
tests reject.

\subsection{Runtime feature provenance}\label{sec:provenance}
What this subsection reports is a limitation rather than a result.

A payload such as \texttt{\{"deviceId":"soil01", "temperature":24.8, "soilMoisture":42.5\}}
contains no flow-derived features at all. Feeding those fields to a model trained on flow records
and reporting a detection outcome would be meaningless: they are application data, the model
consumes network measurements, and they are different objects. The code keeps them apart.

Each message therefore carries two planes, drawn in Fig.~\ref{fig:planes}. The \emph{application
plane} is the payload, and it is what Ascon protects. The \emph{network plane} is a flow-feature
vector drawn from \textbf{held-out records never seen during training} --- the test split of
Section~\ref{sec:data}, 311{,}563 rows --- standing in for the network conditions under which the
message was transmitted, and it is what the detector classifies. The adapter records the
provenance of every feature value the model consumes, and no value is invented. The scenario
control used in evaluation selects \emph{which held-out pool} a record is drawn from; it
synthesises nothing.

We considered two alternatives and rejected both. Synthesising network features with a generative
model cannot be verified and would evaluate the detector against its own generator's artefacts.
Instrumenting a live broker recovers only part of the feature set without the original extractor,
and mixing measured with replayed features introduces a distribution shift we could not document.

The runtime demonstration therefore establishes \emph{architectural correctness}: the pipeline
separates the two planes, routes on the verdict, seals and verifies correctly, and never places a
malicious-verdict payload on the cloud path. It does \emph{not} show that a model trained on this
corpus would detect attacks against a live agricultural deployment. That claim would require
capture and feature re-extraction on the target network, and we do not make it.

\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:setup}
\textbf{Splits and budget.} After deduplication the corpus yields 1{,}237{,}904 training rows
and 311{,}563 held-out test rows, with a realised train/test hash overlap of zero. Unless stated
otherwise every run uses $W = 16$, $\alpha = 0.5$, $R = 20$ rounds, $E = 3$ local epochs, a cap of
400{,}000 sequences per client, and three seeds reported as mean $\pm$ standard deviation. Every
baseline is compute-matched at $R \times E = 60$ local passes, so a difference between them is
attributable to the method rather than to the budget.

\textbf{Metrics.} For the eight-class task we report macro-averaged precision and recall,
\begin{equation}
\text{macro-F1} = \frac{1}{C}\sum_{c=1}^{C} \frac{2 P_c R_c}{P_c + R_c},
\label{eq:macrof1}
\end{equation}
per-class F1, balanced accuracy, the Matthews correlation coefficient and the confusion matrix.
For the binary projection we report precision, recall, PR-AUC and the false-positive rate
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{FPR} = \frac{\mathrm{FP}}{\mathrm{FP} + \mathrm{TN}}.
\label{eq:fpr}
\end{equation}
FPR is first-class because in an operational detector a high false-alarm rate is the failure that
causes the system to be switched off. Accuracy is reported alongside these and never on its own.

\textbf{Provenance of every number.} Each run writes a manifest recording seeds, library versions,
hardware, the full configuration, the realised per-class counts and the leakage-gate result. Every
figure in this paper is generated directly from those manifests by a script, and every number in
the text is read from them; none is transcribed by hand.

\textbf{A stated expectation.} Published results on this corpus routinely exceed 98--99\%
accuracy. Near-ceiling accuracy here is a known property of the data, produced by the imbalance of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ir} and the separability of the flooding classes, and is not evidence of a superior
model. We state in advance that results in that range are expected, and direct attention instead
to macro-F1 on the rare families and to FPR, where differences between methods actually appear.

\subsection{Centralised baselines and the value of recurrence}
Three centralised baselines were trained on an identical split at an identical budget, over
three seeds (Fig.~\ref{fig:baselines}). A random forest on single records reaches macro-F1
$0.6855$; a multilayer perceptron on single records, which differs from the detector only by the
absence of recurrence, reaches $0.6070$; the gated recurrent detector at $W = 16$ reaches
$\mathbf{0.8297 \pm 0.0013}$.

\textbf{Recurrence earns its place.} The gap to the otherwise-matched perceptron is $+0.2322$
macro-F1 --- fifty-one times the seed standard deviation, so it is not a sampling artefact. This
is the ablation the sequence-construction gap demands: because our windows are positional rather
than chronological, the architecture must justify itself empirically rather than by appeal to the
general sequentiality of network traffic. It does.

The random forest result is reported because a reviewed study found a tree ensemble strongest on
tabular flow features~\cite{bai2025ietinfsec}. On single records it is indeed the stronger
non-recurrent model, beating the perceptron by $0.0785$; the recurrent detector beats it by
$0.1442$. The disagreement is informative rather than embarrassing: a tree ensemble is a good
classifier of one record, and the detector's advantage comes from reading sixteen.

\subsection{Federated performance and per-client gain}\label{sec:fedresults}
The three-way comparison the missing-lower-bound gap calls for is given in
Fig.~\ref{fig:bracket}. All three baselines are compute-matched at sixty local passes, so the
comparison isolates the training regime. The federated global model reaches macro-F1
$\mathbf{0.8308 \pm 0.0150}$, sitting inside the bracket its two baselines define: local-only
$0.7205 \pm 0.0750$ below, centralised $0.8543 \pm 0.0040$ above. Federating therefore recovers
$\mathbf{82.4\%}$ of the macro-F1 that keeping data local leaves on the table, for a measured
$815{,}136$ bytes per round.

\textbf{Read the false-positive rate first.} The federated model's FPR is $0.2975 \pm 0.0512$.
Under the imbalance of Eq.~\eqref{eq:ir} that is the number an operator feels, and it is the
reason Section~\ref{sec:setup} declines to lead with accuracy.

\textbf{Local-only is also the least stable baseline} ($\pm 0.0750$ against the federated model's
$\pm 0.0150$), because its score depends entirely on which classes its partition happened to be
dealt.

\textbf{Every farm is better off federating.} Fig.~\ref{fig:gain} gives the per-client gain, which
is the quantity an operator deciding whether to join actually needs. On the mixed-farm
configuration the gains are $+0.1338$, $+0.0789$ and $+0.0516$ macro-F1 on one corpus and
$+0.1568$, $+0.1547$ and $+0.1970$ on the other --- positive for every farm, on every seed, on
both corpora. The sharpest case is a seed in which two clients were dealt partitions missing
attack classes entirely: local-only training fell to $0.6145$ while the federated model still
reached $\mathbf{0.8145}$. A farm that has never seen an attack family can still detect it,
because other farms have, and only parameters crossed the network.

\subsection{Cryptographic and communication overhead}\label{sec:commcost}
Equation~\eqref{eq:bytes} predicts $811{,}200$ bytes per round at $K = 3$; we measure
$815{,}136$, the $0.5\%$ excess being serialisation framing. Per client per direction that is
$135{,}942$ bytes, so $271{,}883$ bytes both ways --- a figure worth stating carefully, because the
aggregate over three clients in one direction ($407{,}752$ bytes) is easily mistaken for one
client's cost.

Set against the alternative, the argument for federation under constraint~C2 is quantitative. A
twenty-round schedule costs roughly $15.5$~MiB in total. Moving the records themselves instead
would be on the order of hundreds of megabytes, a factor of roughly eighteen larger. That ratio
holds \emph{because the model is small}: by Eq.~\eqref{eq:params} it is 33{,}800 parameters, or
132~KiB at single precision. It would not hold for a large model retrained frequently, which is a
reason to prefer a compact detector independently of inference cost.

\textbf{The ratio is also specific to small $K$.} Communication grows linearly in client count
(Fig.~\ref{fig:comm}), from $0.82$~MB per round at $K = 3$ to $\mathbf{13.59}$~MB at $K = 50$, a
$16.6\times$ increase. Since accuracy has already fallen $0.079$ by $K = 50$
(Table~\ref{tab:ksweep}), the benefit of federating degrades before the communication cost becomes
the binding constraint. For this system, client count is limited by rare-class dilution rather
than by bandwidth.

\subsection{Dataset selection as a result}\label{sec:semantics}
Three corpora were characterised and trained on; one was selected. We report the
measurements rather than assert the choice, because two of the rejections are results in
themselves.

\textbf{A medical corpus does not help an agricultural detector.}
CICIoMT2024~\cite{dadkhah2024ciciomt} is the easy case technically: the same extractor family,
the same canonical columns, a genuine drop-in. It still fails on substance. It carries six of the
eight attack families, lacking two entirely, so it adds volume rather than coverage; and the
federated client structure should model the deployment domain, which for this work is farms.

\textbf{A shared column name is not a shared quantity.} Measuring the two corpora's selected
features directly (Fig.~\ref{fig:overlap}) shows that three of the sixteen do not denote the same
thing. \texttt{Variance} spans $0$ to $1.13\times10^{8}$ in one corpus and $0$ to $1$ in the other,
with every row of the second below one. \texttt{Header\_Length} spans $0$ to $60$ --- a correct IP
header, median 20 --- against $0$ to $9.89\times10^{6}$. \texttt{IAT} medians differ by a factor of
$2.4\times10^{11}$. These are three of the four highest-ranked features from
Section~\ref{sec:features}.

Part of the discrepancy has a clean explanation. The packet-count feature differs by
$\mathbf{7.33\times}$ between the corpora ($69.66$ against $9.51$), meaning the two extractors use
different window sizes; normalising the count and sum features per packet restores four of the
eight broken columns to overlap. The remaining three do not recover and must be dropped from any
shared vocabulary. \textbf{This is the cross-corpus semantics gap}, and it is invisible under
per-corpus standardisation, which is why it survived until a cross-corpus evaluation was attempted.

\textbf{A corpus can be good and still be unusable here.} Edge-IIoTset~\cite{ferrag2022edgeiiotset}
is the most agriculture-relevant corpus available, carrying MQTT and Modbus traffic from
soil-moisture, temperature and pH sensing. Forced into the canonical feature language it scores
macro-F1 $0.12$. Trained instead on its own native columns --- eight MQTT fields, two Modbus
fields, and transport flags, lengths and timings --- the same architecture reaches
$\mathbf{0.9822 \pm 0.0043}$ over twelve classes with FPR $0.0017$. The corpus is not the problem;
our translation was.

It is nonetheless excluded, for two reasons. Its value lives in \texttt{mqtt.*} and
\texttt{mbtcp.*} columns that do not exist in the primary corpus's extractor output, and federated
averaging requires an identical input width across clients, so a 26-input detector cannot be
averaged with a 16-input one. A zero-padded union of the two vocabularies is not an escape: the
model learns the zero pattern as corpus identity and silently becomes two sub-models with inflated
in-distribution scores. And measured against the primary corpus's 34 leaf classes, its only
genuinely novel attack type is ransomware. Splitting the architecture to gain one attack family is
not a trade worth making.

Three of its own classes are also inexpressible once identifier columns are removed as leakage
discipline requires: two flood classes contribute one and thirty-eight distinct windows
respectively, because every behavioural feature is literally zero for every packet in them. Their
only distinguishing content lay in transport nonces.

\textbf{No further corpus was added.} The one candidate sharing the primary corpus's feature
family was checked and shares none of its canonical columns. More general-purpose
corpora~\cite{herzalla2023tiissrc} would not deliver zero-shot transfer either, for the reason
Section~\ref{sec:thread_e} gives.

\subsection{Zero-shot cross-corpus transfer: a replication}
Evaluated on a corpus held out entirely from training, the detector collapses: macro-F1
falls to approximately $0.08$ with a false-positive rate above $0.9$. We report this as a
\textbf{replication rather than a finding}.

The field-wide result is unambiguous. Four corpora converted to a \emph{single} standardised
43-feature schema --- an experiment designed precisely to remove schema mismatch as an explanation
--- still lose $56.28\%$ of performance on average under cross-corpus
evaluation~\cite{layeghy2022generalisability}, with scores sometimes near zero and strong asymmetry
between directions. A federated IDS evaluated across three IoT corpora loses up to thirty
percentage points of macro-F1 the same way~\cite{dataset_centric_fl_ids}.

Two consequences follow for this paper. First, \textbf{the semantics defect of
Section~\ref{sec:semantics} is not the cause}: standardising the schema does not rescue transfer
for anyone, so correcting our feature mapping --- which is owed on its own merits --- would not
change this result. Second, zero-shot cross-testbed transfer is not an achievable target, and we
do not treat it as one. The deployment answer is per-site capture and federated adaptation, whose
benefit is the measured per-client gain of Section~\ref{sec:fedresults}: a farm that has never
seen an attack family detects it anyway, because another farm has.

must be read on an ADC1 pin: the ESP32's second ADC is disabled while the Wi-Fi radio is active,
so a node that is permanently associated --- which every node here is --- would read noise from an
ADC2 pin and report it as a moisture value.

\textbf{Gateway placement.} Each gateway runs its own MQTT broker, so sensor readings never leave
the farm on the device-to-gateway hop, and the farm boundary of
Section~\ref{sec:threat} is a physical one rather than a convention. The detector, the verdict
router and the alert sink all run on the same gateway; only the sealed parameter exchange and the
TLS benign path leave it.

\textbf{What changes, and what does not.} Nothing in the software changes. Per node the
deployment requires Python~3.12 or newer --- which rules out distributions still shipping 3.11 ---
the channel key and cloud certificate copied to each gateway, a broker per farm, and the sensor
firmware flashed with that farm's gateway address. Manifests record the platform string, so a
figure produced on hardware can never be confused with one produced in a container.

\textbf{What hardware will measure that this paper cannot.} Three quantities are unavailable
here and are the reason the hardware stage exists: per-round wall-clock time on a gateway-class
CPU, from which the feasible round schedule follows; energy per round and per inference, which
determines whether a solar-powered deployment can sustain the training plane at all; and the
behaviour of the sensor hop over real Wi-Fi rather than a container network, including loss and
reassociation. Everything reported in this paper is architectural, and
Section~\ref{sec:limitations} states that boundary rather than inviting the reader to assume it
away.

\subsection{A safety property can pass vacuously}\label{sec:vacuous}
An earlier run of this same configuration reported the disjointness property satisfied, on
\emph{one} malicious reading: the sensor simulators began publishing before the gateways had
finished federating, and the broker's quality-of-service level discards messages published before
a subscription exists. The assertion held and established nothing. A system leaking one malicious
reading in ten would have passed that test with probability $0.9$; at 4{,}239 readings the same
system passes with probability $1.1\times10^{-194}$.

We therefore state the general form as a gap: \emph{a safety property reported as satisfied
carries no information unless the number of opportunities to violate it is reported alongside.}
We report both throughout.

We state these before a reviewer has to.

\textbf{Detection on live agricultural traffic is not demonstrated.} Under the boundary of
Section~\ref{sec:provenance} the detector classifies held-out flow records paired to each arriving
message; the application payload is never parsed into features. What the runtime results establish
is architectural correctness. Establishing detection on a live deployment requires capture and
feature re-extraction on the target network, which we have not done.

\textbf{Cross-testbed transfer was measured and it failed.} Section~\ref{sec:thread_e} reports why
this is the field-wide result rather than a defect of this system, and why correcting the feature
semantics would not change it. The honest position is that zero-shot transfer is unavailable and
per-site adaptation is the deployment path.

\textbf{Ascon protects the parameter channel, not the telemetry.} The gateway-to-cloud hop uses
TLS. The security claim of this paper is about Channel~3 of Section~\ref{sec:threat}.

\textbf{No on-device results.} Every number here comes from containerised processes at the target
CPU architecture. Container timings are not gateway timings, and no latency or energy claim is
made for field hardware; manifests record the platform so the two cannot be confused.

\textbf{$K = 3$ is a budget constraint, not an optimum.} It is the hardware configuration. The
sweep of Section~\ref{sec:ksweep} is what measures the architecture's behaviour across client
counts, and it happens to show $K = 3$ is also the accuracy-optimal point --- but that is a result,
not the reason the value was chosen.

\textbf{Leave-one-device-out could not be run.} It is the generalisation question a federated farm
actually faces, and Galal~\cite{galal2026provenance} shows it is answerable and discriminating.
Our corpus's published records carry no device attribution --- they are organised by attack type
--- so the experiment is unavailable here. A corpus such as Farm-Flow~\cite{ferreira2025farmflow} would
enable it.

\textbf{Threats the federation does not address.} Gradient inversion and model poisoning are both
live against this design. We claim only the narrower property that raw records are not
transmitted. Differential privacy, secure aggregation and Byzantine-robust aggregation are out of
scope and are not approximated by anything here.

\textbf{A methodological limitation of our own results.} Section~\ref{sec:vacuous} shows that one
of our earlier runs satisfied the disjointness property on a single malicious reading. We report
opportunity counts alongside every safety claim for that reason, and recommend others do the same.

\textbf{Ethics.} The corpus is machine-generated traffic from a controlled testbed, used under its
publishers' terms. No live attack traffic is generated: every malicious instance is a replayed
record, and nothing is directed at any real network. Key material is excluded from version
control and demonstration keys are labelled as such.

\section{Conclusion}
We built a federated intrusion detector for smart agriculture in which the parameter
exchange itself is the protected channel, and measured it.

Three results carry the paper. Federating is worth it \emph{per farm}: every client scored better
federated than alone, on every seed and both corpora, and a client dealt a partition missing
attack classes rose from $0.6145$ to $0.8145$ macro-F1 --- it detected what it had never seen,
because another farm had. The sealed bidirectional channel costs $271{,}883$ bytes per client per
round and rejected nothing across 120 frames. And path disjointness held across 4{,}239 malicious
readings with none reaching the cloud, cross-checked against the receiver's own count.

Two results are negative and we report them as contributions. Zero-shot cross-corpus transfer
collapses, replicating a documented field-wide failure that standardised feature schemas do not
fix. And a safety property can pass vacuously: an earlier run of ours satisfied the disjointness
assertion on one malicious reading, which a system leaking one in ten would have passed nine times
in ten. A guarantee reported as satisfied carries no information unless the number of
opportunities to violate it is reported with it.

The client-count sweep adds a third observation with a methodological edge. Aggregate macro-F1
degrades gently to $K = 50$, falling $0.079$; the weakest class falls $0.256$ over the same range,
a $40\%$ relative collapse, and no client is ever empty. Reporting the aggregate alone would have
concealed it.

What remains is the work the boundary of Section~\ref{sec:provenance} defines. A live feature
extractor on the gateway, capture on the target network, and on-device validation would convert
the architectural claim into a detection claim. The federated structure is what makes that
tractable: each farm contributes what it captures, and the others receive it as parameters.
